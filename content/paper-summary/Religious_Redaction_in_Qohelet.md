**Paper Title:** Religious Redaction in Qohelet in Light of Mesopotamian Vanity Literature  
**Link to Paper:** https://doi.org/10.1163/15685330-12301225  
**Date:** 2016  
**Paper Type:** Biblical Studies, Mesopotamian Wisdom Literature, Textual Criticism  
**Short Abstract:**  
In this paper, Nili Samet argues that the pious epilogue of Qohelet (Ecclesiastes) is a later interpolation. Since this theory rests only on internal evidence, she brings external support from Mesopotamian texts: scribes there reworked "vanity" literature toward conservative, religious readings for two millennia, so conservative redaction of vanity texts was a real, recurring practice.

## The Problem of Qohelet's Epilogue

Qohelet is a skeptical book. Its author argues that everything is vanity, that human deeds are worthless, and that the only sensible response is to enjoy life while it lasts. Scattered through the book, and especially at its end, stand pious statements that clash with this message. The epilogue (12:9-14) refers to Qohelet in the third person, praises him as a sage, and closes with a postscript: fear God and keep his commandments.

The standard critical explanation is that this epilogue was added by a later redactor to reconcile the book with orthodoxy. The evidence is internal: the change of person, the change of tone, and the apologetic reading of Qohelet's provocations as a deliberate educational method (like a shepherd's goad) that fits Qohelet badly. The problem is that all of this is deduction from the final text alone. Some scholars, most prominently Michael Fox, reject the theory and attribute the whole book, epilogue included, to one author who writes in different voices. Samet's answer to them is simple: if the epilogue's message were the point of the book, why bury it under a book of vanity? And if the vanity is the point, why end with a conservative postscript?

Her way out of this deadlock is comparative. Following Jeffery Tigay's "empirical models" methodology, she uses texts whose earlier stages are actually documented, in multiple manuscripts and versions, to show that the kind of redaction posited for Qohelet really happened elsewhere.

## The Vanity Theme in Mesopotamian Literature

Discoveries of the last decades have established that the theme of vanity was old and central in Mesopotamian wisdom. Bendt Alster coined the term for a group of Sumerian works from the second millennium BCE, and the theme continues in Akkadian texts of the first millennium. Six compositions are known in which life is fleeting, achievements are worthless, and enjoyment of the present is the only rational response: the Ballad of Early Rulers, Niĝnam ("everything is worthless"), Enlil and Namzitara, the Dialogue of Pessimism, the Counsels of a Pessimist, and Šima Milka, plus vanity passages in the Gilgamesh epic. The texts share themes and sometimes even phraseology. Crucially for Samet's argument, several of them survive in multiple versions, which makes their editorial history visible.

## Niĝnam: Religious Reinterpretation in Sumer

The Sumerian poem Niĝnam opens with the couplet: "Everything is worthless, but life is good; when a man does not have wealth—he has wealth." Version A draws the expected carpe diem conclusion: wisdom cannot pierce the limits of the cosmos (the tallest man cannot reach heaven), so one should enjoy joy and one's good house instead of philosophical inquiry.

Version D is an expansion of A with the same opening and closing, but its heart redirects the message. Whatever a man possesses is a gift of his personal god; if the god looks favorably on him, his protective deities dwell in his body and his offerings never cease. The vanity riddle is now answered with religious devotion and proper cult rather than hedonism. Version C is similar in tendency, ending with the couplet that for the one who gives food to the gods, life is found. This is, as far as the evidence goes, the oldest known religious adaptation of a vanity text, already at the start of the second millennium BCE.

## The Ballad of Early Rulers: A Social-Justice Appendix

The Ballad of Early Rulers lists glorious dead kings whose grandeur is now forgotten, and concludes that one should enjoy the present. It circulated widely, was translated into Akkadian, and was copied at Emar and Ugarit in the Late Bronze Age. One Ugarit manuscript, however, appends lines with no parallel in the other versions: man's life-span is known only to the gods, so one should not insult others, not scorn the weak, and remember that the cripple may overtake the runner and the rich man may end up begging from the poor.

The appendix does not follow from the poem's message; it replaces carpe diem with prudence and social justice, ideas at home in traditional wisdom rather than in vanity literature. The redactor's agenda is conservative here too, only ethical rather than religious.

## The Dialogue of Pessimism: Censorship by Omission

The Dialogue of Pessimism, probably from the eighth or seventh century BCE, is the most corrosive of the vanity texts. In one stanza the master considers sacrificing to his god, and the slave first approves, then dissuades him: accustom the god to offerings and he will run after you like a dog, demanding rites and more rites. Comparing the personal god to an irksome dog is among the most blatant blasphemies in the piece.

The Dialogue is known from five manuscripts, four from seventh-century Assyria and one from Babylon whose peculiarities point to a late, Seleucid-period date. In that late manuscript, the blasphemous line is missing. Samet suggests the omission is deliberate: the only surviving witness of a late scribal attempt to restrain the dialogue's message.

## Qohelet and Gilgamesh: God Inserted into a Secular Speech

The closest literary connection between Qohelet and Mesopotamia is the parallel between the ale-wife Šiduri's speech in the Old Babylonian Gilgamesh epic and Qohelet 9:7-9. Six components appear in the same order in both: enjoy your food, an emphasis of always or day-and-night, clean garments, a washed head, enjoyment with a wife, and the closing statement that this is the human lot. Samet counts this sequence as evidence of literary borrowing, not coincidence.

The differences are then telling. The Hebrew text inserts a line with no Akkadian equivalent: "for God has already approved what you do." The carpe diem advice is now carried out with divine approval, and the closing phrase makes God the giver of the vain life. It cannot be determined when or by whom these insertions were made; the change could have entered at any link in the chain of transmission between Old Babylonian Gilgamesh and the final redactors of Qohelet. Either way, the pattern is the same one documented above: a religious overlay on an originally secular vanity passage.

## Counsels of a Pessimist: The Case with Missing Evidence

The Counsels of a Pessimist survives as a single fragment. Its preserved portion states that whatever men do does not last forever, then jumps to conservative admonitions: offer prayers to your god, bow to your city goddess, care for your livestock. The jump looks exactly like the adaptations discussed above, but with only one manuscript there is no way to confirm that the conservative ending is secondary. The case awaits new finds.

## Conclusion

The comparison shows a recurring pattern rather than a local accident. Vanity literature conflicted with traditional values from the moment it appeared, and scribes responded by softening or reinterpreting it, in Sumerian, in Akkadian, and apparently also in Hebrew, across two millennia and many places. Samet is careful about what this proves: the Mesopotamian parallels do not demonstrate that the epilogue of Qohelet is an interpolation, but they give the redaction theory an external foothold it previously lacked. Combined with the internal arguments, they make the theory highly probable, which in biblical criticism, where the earlier stages of the text are hidden, is about as much as can be hoped for.
