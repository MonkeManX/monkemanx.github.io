**Paper Title:** Mindfulness, Cognition, and Long-Term Meditators: Toward a Science of Advanced Meditation

**Link to Paper:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40800948/

**Authors:** Ehmann S., Sezer I., Treves I.N., Gabrieli J.D.E., Sacchet M.D.

**Date:** 2025 (Imaging Neuroscience, Volume 3)

**Paper Type:** Review, Mindfulness Meditation, Cognitive Neuroscience, Neurophenomenology

**Short Abstract:**
This review looks at what happens to the brain and behavior when people meditate for a very long time (at least 1,500 hours). Long-term meditators seem to get better at noticing what their body feels, feel less bothered by pain, make more rational decisions, stay more emotionally neutral, and have a more flexible sense of self. Brain scans show they use different brain networks than beginners, more bottom-up processing and less top-down control. The authors argue that we need better ways to study really advanced meditators (not just people who have done it for a long time) by combining what they report feeling with brain data.

## Introduction

Mindfulness meditation comes from ancient Buddhist teachings, especially the Satipatthāna, which talks about two skills: *sati* (mindfulness) and *sampajañña* (clear comprehension). In the West, Kabat-Zinn made it popular by defining it as "paying attention in the present moment, on purpose, without judging." This led to programs like MBSR that help with depression, anxiety, chronic pain, and addiction.

This review uses a simple three-part model of mindfulness by Young (2016):
- **Concentration**: focusing on one thing
- **Sensory Clarity**: noticing the fine details of what you experience
- **Equanimity**: staying non-reactive to whatever comes up

The authors make an important distinction. A **long-term meditator** just has many hours of practice. But an **advanced meditator** has actual skill, they can enter deep states like jhana (absorptive concentration) or experience non-dual awareness. The authors think studying the second group is where the real insights are.

## Methods

This is a review paper, the authors didn't run new experiments. They collected existing studies on long-term meditators, defined as people with at least 1,500 hours of practice (roughly 1 hour a day for 5 years). They grouped the findings into four areas: perception, emotions, decision-making, and unusual states of consciousness.

## Cognitive Functions in Long-Term Meditators

**Perception.** Long-term meditators seem better at feeling what's happening inside their bodies, but this doesn't work equally for all body signals. The most consistent finding is about pain: they still feel the same *intensity* of pain, but it bothers them *less*. They've learned to separate the raw sensation from the emotional reaction. Brain scans show this happens because they use different brain regions, less prefrontal cortex (thinking/evaluating) and more salience network (noticing/accepting).

**Emotions.** Long-term meditators are more emotionally neutral, negative things don't upset them as much, and compassion meditation can actually boost positive feelings. Over time, their brain shifts from actively controlling emotions (top-down, using the prefrontal cortex) to a more automatic, accepting style (bottom-up, with changes in the default mode network). This fits with the idea that they just let emotions be instead of fighting them.

**Decision-Making.** In an economic game (the Ultimatum Game), meditators accept unfair offers more often, they're more rational and less driven by the emotional impulse to punish unfairness. Their brain activity suggests they rely more on body awareness and less on cognitive control during these decisions. Another study found they set higher "decision thresholds," meaning they gather more information before committing to a choice.

**Altered States of Consciousness.** Long-term meditators are better at entering states where the sense of self dissolves, time feels different (stretched or gone), and effort drops away. These experiences are linked to reduced activity in the default mode network (especially the posterior cingulate cortex) and changes in the temporoparietal junction. Practices that involve "observing and releasing" (letting go of control) are best at triggering these states.

## Limitations

The studies in this review have several problems. Sample sizes are small, meditation hours and techniques are reported inconsistently (which makes comparisons hard), and almost no studies collected first-person reports that would distinguish long-term practice from true advanced skill. Cultural context (like which tradition someone practices in) is mostly ignored. The authors suggest the field needs unified frameworks, better measurement tools, and preregistered longitudinal studies.
