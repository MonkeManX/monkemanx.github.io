I have some reservations about the overuse of strict logical arguments in philosophy, especially the common structure of "premise, premise, conclusion." This approach works extremely well in mathematics, where the objects of reasoning are precisely defined and where symbols have fixed meanings. However, natural language does not work in this way.

Words do not have perfectly fixed definitions that exist independently of their use. Rather, we learn the meaning of words by participating in a linguistic community: by observing how words are used in different situations and by developing a network of associations connected to them. A word is therefore not like a mathematical symbol with a single precise meaning, but more like a cloud of related meanings, associations, and intuitions.

Because of this, philosophical arguments that rely too heavily on formal logical structures can sometimes become misleading. It is often easy to find an apparent flaw in an argument by taking one association of a word used in a premise and applying it in a way that goes against the intended direction of the argument. The problem is not necessarily that the argument is wrong, but that the word itself contains multiple overlapping meanings.

For example, morality can be understood both as something emotional and intuitive, connected to feelings such as empathy and guilt, and as something rational, connected to principles and reasoning. God can be understood both as a personal being with intentions and as an impersonal foundation of reality. Time can be understood both categorically, as a fundamental structure of reality, and relationally, as something dependent on observers and physical systems. Many philosophical disagreements arise because different sides emphasize different aspects of the same concept.

For this reason, I think philosophy should not rely only on constructing strict logical arguments. Language is not merely a tool for representing precisely defined concepts; it is also a tool for shaping perception. In this sense, language is perhaps closer to painting than to mathematics. A painter does not communicate by giving a strict definition of an object but by arranging colours and forms in a way that guides the viewer's perception. Similarly, a philosopher often uses words not simply to transfer information but to direct attention toward a particular way of seeing the world.

Philosophical writing is therefore less like proving a mathematical theorem and more like creating a conceptual landscape. The goal is not always to move from premises toward a single unavoidable conclusion, but rather to guide the reader into a region of understanding where certain connections and insights become visible.

Just as a painter refines a painting by adjusting individual strokes until the overall image conveys what they intend, a philosopher refines language word by word until the meaning they want to express becomes clearer. The meaning does not arise from one isolated definition, but from the relationship between all the elements together.
