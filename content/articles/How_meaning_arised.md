When we look at different people, we can see how deeply they engage with certain activities and how varied these activities can be.

Some people love chess. They enjoy playing it, watching it, and exploring its rich landscape. Names like Magnus Carlsen, Bobby Fischer, Garry Kasparov, and Hikaru Nakamura mean something to them. So do openings like the London System and the King's Indian Defence.

Others are passionate about mathematics. They know mathematicians like Grigori Perelman and Terence Tao, and they find meaning in mathematical ideas like Fermat's Last Theorem and the Riemann hypothesis. They might also appreciate the techniques used in mathematical proofs, such as induction or introducing auxiliary terms to simplify an expression.

Others are deeply invested in football. They have a favourite club and favourite players. They know the tactics their teams use, remember famous matches, and follow the stories surrounding the sport.

Some people devote themselves to poetry. They know their favourite poets and poems, have preferences for particular metres, such as dactyls or trochees, and enjoy specific forms, such as limericks or sonnets.

Finally, there is something particularly meaningful to me: philosophy. People who engage deeply with philosophy develop their own preferences for philosophers like Kant and Heidegger. They have favourite philosophical problems and theories, and they know the details and anecdotes surrounding the lives of philosophers. They might know, for example, about Schopenhauer's misogyny and his alleged act of pushing a woman down the stairs, while also knowing that he regarded compassion as the foundation of morality.

For people outside these fields, however, much of this knowledge remains superficial. They might recognise a few names or have heard of some of these concepts, but they lack the deeper familiarity and attachment that make them meaningful to those who engage with them. Some might not even have heard of them. More importantly, they might not understand what makes these activities worth pursuing in the first place.

A natural question arises: **How can there be so many different fields that are deeply meaningful and engaging to some people, yet hold little or no meaning for others?** Or in other words, how does meaning arise in the first place?

Perhaps, if we can answer this question, we can approach a more personal one: **What is meaningful to me, and what should I find meaningful?**

I have been thinking about this for a while and have tried to express these thoughts in several articles, including [Aristotle's Virtue Ethics](https://monkemanx.github.io/articles/aristotele_virtue_ethics/), [How to Live](https://monkemanx.github.io/articles/how_to_live/), and [Where the Eiffel Tower Is Is Not Objective](https://monkemanx.github.io/articles/where_the_eiffel_tower_is_is_not_obejctive/). Yet whenever I try to articulate these ideas, my language becomes muddy and unclear.

1. How does something become intelligible?
Heidegger, Wittgenstein and Gadamer explain how our practical involvement, language, training and interpretative background shape what we can understand and notice.

2. How does an intelligible activity become meaningful?
Your Augustine paper and MacIntyre help explain how an activity acquires significance through its place in a framework of practices, purposes, standards and internal goods.

3. Which meaningful activities are worth pursuing?
Susan Wolf and Charles Taylor address the evaluative question. Isaiah Berlin challenges the assumption that all valuable pursuits must fit into one ideal life.
